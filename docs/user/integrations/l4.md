# L4 and Logical English

*Kind: integration guide · Audience: users · Status: current (2026-09-30)*

L4 is a language that writes laws and contracts as programs. The Centre for
Computational Law of Singapore Management University (SMU) made L4. Now the
company Legalese develops it. An L4 program has two halves:

- **rules**, which tell what follows from what: `DECIDE` a person
  `is eligible` `IF` the person is over 18 `AND` …;
- **contracts**, which tell who must do what, who can do what, and who must
  not do what, and by when: `PARTY` the seller `MUST` deliver the goods
  `WITHIN` 14 days `HENCE` … `LEST` …

Logical English (LE) reads the two halves. **File ▸ Open…** (or **File ▸ Import
from Another System…**) accepts an `.l4` file. The rules become an ordinary
Logical English program. The tests of the program become scenarios. The
contracts become a program in **Logical English for LPS**. Logical English for
LPS is the form of Logical English for rules about time and change. The LPS2
editor runs it. LPS is an abbreviation for Logic Production System. The
translator is a part of the InsurLE extensions. Thus only the installations
that have the extensions give the translator, for example the hosted service.
Every installation has the translations of the examples of L4. These
translations have the name *twins*.

## L4 in brief: a small example

The file below, `refund.l4`, is a full L4 program. It has one rule, one
contract and four tests.

```l4
§ `Refunds`

DECLARE Purchase HAS
    `buyer's age`         IS A NUMBER
    `days since purchase` IS A NUMBER

GIVEN p IS A Purchase
GIVETH A BOOLEAN
DECIDE p `gives a right to a refund` IF
        p's `days since purchase` <= 30
    OR  p's `buyer's age` >= 65

DECLARE Party IS ONE OF Buyer, Seller
DECLARE Action IS ONE OF `return the goods`, `refund` HAS amount IS A NUMBER

GIVETH A DEONTIC Party Action
`refund process` MEANS
    PARTY Buyer
    MAY `return the goods`
    WITHIN 30
    HENCE
        PARTY Seller
        MUST refund 100
        WITHIN 14
        LEST BREACH BY Seller BECAUSE "did not refund"

#EVAL (Purchase WITH `buyer's age` IS 40, `days since purchase` IS 10) `gives a right to a refund`
#EVAL (Purchase WITH `buyer's age` IS 40, `days since purchase` IS 45) `gives a right to a refund`
#TRACE `refund process` AT 0 WITH
    PARTY Buyer DOES `return the goods` AT 5
    PARTY Seller DOES refund 100 AT 12
#TRACE `refund process` AT 0 WITH
    PARTY Buyer DOES `return the goods` AT 5
    PARTY Seller DOES refund 100 AT 25
```

Read the program from the top:

- `§` starts a section. The words in backquotes are a name. A name in
  backquotes can be a full phrase.
- `DECLARE Purchase HAS …` declares a **record**. A purchase has two fields:
  the age of the buyer, and the days since the purchase. The two fields are
  numbers.
- `GIVEN` gives the names of the inputs of the next part. `GIVETH` tells the
  type of value that the part gives. Here, the value is yes or no (a
  `BOOLEAN`).
- `DECIDE … IF …` is a **rule**. A purchase gives a right to a refund if it is
  30 days old or less. It also gives a right to a refund if the buyer is 65 or
  older. The indentation groups the `OR`. The name
  `gives a right to a refund` comes after its input, `p`. Thus the rule reads
  as a sentence. L4 calls a name among its inputs *mixfix*.
- `IS ONE OF` declares a **choice**. The parties are the buyer and the seller.
  The actions are the return of the goods and a refund. A refund has an
  amount.
- `` `refund process` MEANS … `` defines a **contract**. A contract is a value
  of the L4 type `DEONTIC`. The buyer *may* return the goods within 30 days.
  `HENCE` tells what follows when the buyer does that: the seller *must*
  refund 100 within 14 days. `LEST` tells what follows when the seller does not
  refund: a breach, by the seller, with the reason.
- `#EVAL` asks L4 to calculate a value. It is a test. `#TRACE` runs the
  contract against events on given days. The first trace ends fulfilled. In the
  second trace, the refund comes on day 25. That is after the deadline of day
  19, thus the contract ends breached by the seller.

When you open the file in this editor, the rule becomes Logical English:

```le
the functions are:
    the age of the buyer of *a purchase* is *a number*.
    the days since purchase of *a purchase* is *a number*.

the templates are:
    *a purchase* gives a right to a refund.

the knowledge base refund includes:

a purchase gives a right to a refund if
    the days since purchase of the purchase is a number N
    and N <= 30
    or the age of the buyer of the purchase is a number M
        and M >= 65.

scenario line_27 is, as stated in the source program at line 27:
    the age of the buyer of purchase_1 is 40.
    the days since purchase of purchase_1 is 10.
    line_27 expects answers ["purchase_1 gives a right to a refund"].
```

The contract becomes a Logical English for LPS program,
`refund_contracts.le`. In that program, each step of the contract is a fluent:

```le
when a party does return the goods on day a day
    and refund process permits the party to return the goods by day a due day as its first obligation
    and the day <= the due day
    and a second due day = day + 14
then refund process obliges seller to refund with amount 100 by day the second due day as its second obligation
    and it is not the case that refund process permits the party to return the goods by day the due day as its first obligation.
```

The two programs give the same answers as L4. The two `#EVAL`s are
scenarios. The two `#TRACE`s are runs in the LPS2 editor, and these runs end
as the runs of L4 end.

## At a glance

| Direction | Where (menu item) | Files | What you get | Checked against |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L4 → Logical English | **File ▸ Open…**, **File ▸ Import from Another System…** | `.l4` | the rules as Logical English, the `#EVAL` and `#ASSERT` tests of the file as scenarios, a ledger; the contracts as a Logical English for LPS program, written adjacent to it | the evaluator of L4: 621 of 621 answers on 62 twins |
| L4 → Logical English for LPS | the same menu items in the LPS2 editor | `.l4` | the contracts: each obligation is a fluent, each `#TRACE` is a scenario; the rules written adjacent to it | the evaluator of L4: 87 of the 95 traces of the L4 examples end at the same point as in L4, and none are different |

There is no way back yet. The editor does not write L4.

## How to use it

### Opening an L4 file

Do these steps:

1. Select **File ▸ Open…**.
2. Select the `.l4` file.

What opens depends on the editor:

- **In this editor**, the rules open. If the file also has contracts, the
  editor writes a second program adjacent to the first,
  `<name>_contracts.le`. The note below the menu bar gives its name. To run
  the contracts, open the same L4 file in the LPS2 editor.
- **In the LPS2 editor**, the contracts open, and the editor writes the rules
  adjacent to them. A file with no contract opens as its rules in the two
  editors.

The note below the menu bar gives the counts of the ledger of the translator:

- how many parts of the file have an exact translation (*encoded*);
- how many parts have a wording that the translator had to make
  (*approximated*);
- how many parts have no translation (*residue*).

The translator never removes a residue. The residue stays in the program as a
comment block that starts with the word TODO. The block has the L4 text and the
reason. Thus you or the Contract Assistant can complete it. **File ▸ Show the
Original** shows the L4 file. The editor keeps the L4 file in `sources/`,
adjacent to the program.

### L4's examples

The twins of the L4 examples are in the examples, in
`examples/migration/l4/`. They are:

- the foundation course;
- the legal examples: the British Nationality Act, an imaginary alcohol act,
  anti-social behaviour notices, a performance award for a chief executive,
  New York environmental review, and the promissory note;
- the regulatory course;
- the reference examples;
- the Jersey charities law.

Each folder keeps its L4 source in `sources/`, with the licence of L4 (Apache
2.0).

If the file in a folder has contracts, the folder also holds a **history
view**, `<name>_history.le` ([below](#the-history-view)). The contract twins
that run in time are in the examples of the LPS2 editor, in a folder with the
same name.

### Where the expected answers come from

Each expected answer of the scenarios of a twin is the answer that the
**evaluator of L4** gives on the same file. The authors of L4 wrote the
evaluator. The evaluator is compiled to WebAssembly. WebAssembly is a form of
program that runs in a browser or in Node.js. The evaluator runs adjacent to
the translator.

- An `#EVAL` or `#ASSERT` line becomes a query. It also becomes a scenario that
  expects the answer of the evaluator.
- A `#TRACE` becomes a scenario with the events of the trace on their days.
  The result of L4 is above the scenario. The result tells if the contract
  ended fulfilled, or ended breached, or which parts stay open.
- Many legal examples have no tests. The reason is that they declare their
  facts with `ASSUME`, and the evaluator cannot run them. For such a file, the
  translator writes the file again, and gives each assumed fact a value. Then
  it asks the evaluator for the answers. When a file has a sufficiently small
  number of yes-or-no facts, the translator tries every combination. This
  gave 152 answers for the alcohol act, and 128 for the anti-social behaviour
  notices.

When you open your own file on a server that does not have the evaluator, the
twin has its scenarios but no expected answers. The note tells you this.

## How L4's rules map to Logical English

L4 is a programming language in which each definition is a function. The
translator changes each function into a sentence pattern, which is a
**template**. It changes the definition into rules.

| L4 | Logical English |
|---|---|
| `DECIDE x IF …` (a yes-or-no question) | a rule that concludes the template of `x` |
| `x MEANS …` (a value) | a template that ends with `… is *a value*`, declared below `the functions are:`, and a rule that gives the value |
| the name of a function | the wording of the template. This is the `@nlg` annotation if there is one. If not, it is a mixfix name with its inputs in their positions (`` p `is eligible for` q `` → `*a person* is eligible for *a program*`). If not, it is the name as a phrase. If not, it is the name with its inputs added at the end; the ledger marks this wording for review |
| `DECLARE Person HAS age IS A NUMBER, …` (a record) | one template for each field, `the age of *a person* is *a number*`; a record that the tests make becomes a named individual, with one fact for each field |
| `DECLARE Colour IS ONE OF Red, Green` (a choice) | named individuals |
| `ASSUME` a fact | a template with the mark `; scenario element`; the scenarios state it |
| `IF … THEN … ELSE`, `BRANCH`, `CONSIDER … WHEN …` | an `otherwise` cascade (reference §17.2) |
| `WHERE` and `LET` (definitions local to a function) | a separate template, with the inputs of the enclosing function as places |
| `AND`, `OR`, `NOT`, arithmetic, comparisons, `MODULO` | `and`, `or`, `it is not the case that`, the same arithmetic, `mod` |
| a function that calls itself | a rule that uses itself |
| `all` and `any` over a list | `for all cases in which …` and an ordinary condition |
| `sum`, `length` of a list | `sum of each … such that`, `count of each … such that` |
| `map` and `filter` kept as a list | `the list of each … such that` (reference §5) |
| dates (`daydate`) | the library `lib/l4_daydate.le`, which counts days as L4 does |
| `§` sections | `section … is:` |
| `@ref`, `@desc` | a citation of the source, a comment |

Items that have no Logical English form stay as residue. These items are text
conversions, web calls, a function that goes to a different function as a
value, and a record that a rule makes.

## How L4's contracts map to Logical English for LPS

The LPS2 documentation has the full description
([L4 and LPS](https://lps2.logicalcontracts.com/docs/user/integrations/l4)).
In summary, each obligation, permission or prohibition is a **fluent**. A
fluent is a fact that is true for a period of time. An example is
`the sale contract obliges the seller to deliver the goods by day 14 as its first obligation`.
Each action of the L4 traces is an **event** with its day. One law
tells what starts next when a party meets the obligation (the L4 `HENCE`).
A different law tells what starts after a missed deadline (the L4 `LEST`).

## The history view

You can read a contract in two ways. The LPS2 editor runs it **forward**. The
days pass, events occur, and the parties meet or miss the deadlines. The
timeline shows this. The **history view** reads the contract **backward**. All
the events have occurred, and the question is the status of the contract.
Usually, people ask a lawyer this question. They ask which obligations the
parties met, and which they missed. They also ask who is in breach, and what
is still due on the last day.

The L4 translator writes the history view itself. No person edits it. The
translator writes it from the same translation of the contract as the LPS twin.
Thus it has the same obligations, in the same words. For each obligation, the
history view has these rules:

- a rule for the day when the obligation **starts**, and its deadline. For the
  first obligation, this is the start of the contract. For the next
  obligation, it is the day when a party met the obligation before it (the L4
  `HENCE`), or missed it (the L4 `LEST`);
- a rule for the day when a party **meets** the obligation. This is the first
  event that matches the action, on or before the deadline;
- a rule for the day when a party **misses** the obligation. This is the first
  event after the deadline, because L4 sees a missed deadline only at the next
  event;
- a rule that tells that the obligation is **still open** at the end of the
  history.

Each `#TRACE` of the L4 file becomes a scenario that gives a list of the
events. Each scenario expects the result of the run of the same trace in the
LPS twin. LE examined that run against the evaluator of L4 first. Thus the
expected answers are the answers of L4. Two traces of the L4 examples keep no
expectation. The file tells the reason:

- the wedding of the foundation course has two events on the same day. A
  history that reads by days cannot put these events in order;
- the history of twelve payments of the promissory note takes more time than
  the test runner permits.

The four rules are the same for each obligation. Thus they could be a library,
`lib/deontic_history.le`, which each history view includes. But the translator
writes them for each obligation, because the library was much slower. The
library needs one sentence, `the obligation that *a sentence* starts on *a date*`,
for each type of obligation. One type of obligation starts when a party
misses a different obligation, and that is a negation. Thus the same sentence
depends on its own negation. Logicians call such a program *unstratified*,
and the system gives a warning about it. Logical English can still run the
program. But it cannot calculate the answers one type at a time, and each
question makes it calculate the full history again. With one sentence for each
obligation, the program is stratified. On the twelve payments of the promissory
note, the written rules take approximately 9 seconds. The library did not
finish after 60 seconds.

## What L4 does that surprises readers

The evaluator of L4, not its documentation, decides the meaning of an L4
program. The twins copy the evaluator of the L4 repository at commit `5e082cf`
(29 September 2026; a later L4 can be different). This includes five
behaviours that possibly a reader of the L4 text does not expect:

- **L4 sees a missed deadline only when the next event occurs.** A contract
  with a deadline that passed without events stays open until the next event.
  That event then starts the item that the `LEST` branch gives. The event does
  not count for the new obligation.
- **Each deadline counts from the time when its obligation started.**
  `WITHIN 30` after a payment means 30 days after that payment, not after the
  start of the contract. The promissory note calculates its instalments as days
  after the start of the loan. Thus its deadlines move more with each payment.
- **The payment of the penalty of the promissory note ends the full note.**
  The `HENCE` of the penalty obligation is `FULFILLED`. Thus the parties do
  not owe the remaining instalments.
- **A name inside an action binds, and does not compare.** In
  `MUST pay amount`, `amount` is an input of the contract. Any payment meets
  the obligation, and `amount` gets the value of the payment. Only `EXACTLY`
  compares the action with the value of the input.
- **A permission that a party does not use still leads to the next step.** In
  the example above, possibly the buyer never returns the goods. The evaluator
  of L4 still starts the obligation of the seller to refund, after the 30 days
  and the next event. But the documentation of L4 tells that an unused
  permission ends the contract as fulfilled. The twins follow the evaluator,
  which gives the answers of L4.

## What is not supported yet

The translator keeps in the program the parts that it cannot translate, as
residue. A residue is a comment block that starts with TODO, with the L4 text
and the reason. In the rules of the L4 examples, 116 of approximately 1,780
parts of the files are residue. (The rules programs mark 43 more parts as
residue. These parts are the contracts, which the contracts program
translates.) The residue is in these groups:

- **Values that have no form in Logical English:** text functions (to join
  texts, or to change a number into text), conversions, web calls (`FETCH`,
  `POST`), and the reading and writing of JSON. JSON is a data format of the
  web.
- **Records and alternatives that a rule makes.** A rule that makes a new
  record (`Money WITH Currency IS "USD", Value IS …`) or an `EITHER` value has
  no form in Logical English. LE translates the records that the tests make,
  as named individuals. For the same reason, a test with a record as its answer
  keeps no expected answer. An example is the amounts of the promissory note.
- **Functions that go to other functions as values.** This does not include
  the small functions written in position for `all`, `any`, `map` and
  `filter`.
- **Lists that a program makes one element at a time**, with `FOLLOWED BY`,
  and `TRUNC` with a number of decimal places.
- **Contracts.** LE does not translate 8 of the 95 traces of the L4 examples.
  7 are in the performance award for a chief executive. That example takes
  lists apart with `FOLLOWED BY`. It also reads a field of a value that is
  known only when the contract runs. The eighth is in the New York
  environmental review. That example applies a function to fewer inputs than
  the function takes. Each of these traces stays as a scenario, with its
  reason. LE translates `RAND` and `ROR`, but not one inside the other.
- **Programs in more than one file.** You must open these programs one file at
  a time.
- **The way back.** The editor does not write L4.
- **The newest syntax of L4.** The translator uses L4 as it was on 29
  September 2026.

## Traps

- **LE changes the words that Logical English keeps for itself.** A template
  cannot contain the words that LE uses for its own sentences (`if`, `and`,
  `or`, `otherwise`, `unless` …). For the L4 name
  `` `is an individual aged 16 or over` ``, the translator writes
  `is an individual aged 16 else over`. The ledger marks each such wording
  for review.
- **Two definitions with sentences that a reader could mix.** The translator
  makes them different with a word such as `given` or `variant two`. The
  ledger records which word.
- **A program in more than one L4 file.** You must open it one file at a
  time. The evaluator of L4 gives the expected answers, and it accepts only
  one file.
- **L4 changes quickly.** The translator uses the L4 repository as it was on
  29 September 2026. LE reads older files, in a syntax that L4 does not accept
  now, as much as possible. The parts that LE cannot read are residue.
